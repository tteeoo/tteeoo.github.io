# Caravaggio and the Crab

<i>A short story</i>

Thrill finds everyone, some way or another. The arbiters of the forces that be penetrate all of time and all of space, weaving the lives of their subjects.

## 0. Prologue

Like an artist without a discipline. Like a hill without a precipice.
I walk the ridge every day in awe of the foggy greatness just outside my small home. Wind whips brownish tall grasses, and sometimes just docily brushes them. Sometimes all of the grass just stands upright, unperterbed. The mornings when I walk, the fog covers everything, such that you can't see below the ridge where settlement lays, or anywhere higher either: an expanse that is limited yet not uncoverable. The walk is a wander and a basking-in. There is little visible to set destination to, nor much novel at any corner, and yet the brownish tall grasses and the fog bring joy. I feel I find myself the same place inside as out.

## 1. The Descent

The humors comprise Blood, the Yellow, the Black, and Phlegm. Phlegm, Margot says, is what I have too much of. She comes in:
"Markus, yesterday I went down to he port and bought from a merchant this wonderful paintbrush. You have 48 days unil I expect the Holy Geist to come down upon you, reinvoke your passions, and you to have used it."
She places the wooden utensil on my desk and exits. Yesterday was Easter and only the third Easter I have spent outside of my mother's company. She thinks I have that great disease, consumption, and has put me to waste away in this shack at the top of the hill outside of town, passively hoping the sunlight, or the gentle winds will bring me back to life. This has been the case for the whole past winter, and now, the spring. My colleagues, or rivals, no longer comunicate. Three weeks ago saw the last letter Margot relayed to me from Janus, stating his plans to journey to Rome, and learn anatomy from the statues there. Everyone thought I would be going to Rome around now. The greatest baroque prodigy of the Duchy, they said. But last autumn I went on a trip. I went on a nice and decent trip, traveling East. And when I got back, I was no more painterly, no more capable of portrayal.

The Phlegm, my maid Margot says, is too prolific in relation to the other three Humors. It is held in my lungs, which my mother thinks are being eaten by that great disease. Previous patrons of mine, who have conspired with them to fund this long artist's retreat, create their own theories as well. Many think that, due to my youthful brashness and licentious proclivities, I consumed some Eastern product that has imbibed my lungs with harm. The truth is, I may have had some seasonal ailment many months ago, but now, I feel perfectly fine.


Yes, my body is young and in a healthful state. As for my lapse in creation, I suppose my former patrons are the most accurate. I realize I should tell you, the reader, the truth after all. That way instead of hiding behind opinions I can at least have attempted to accomplish something.

The trip which was my fall, in which I said goodbye to my countrymen and told them I would travel east to the Crown of Poland, for leisure and to explore pigments there, was a farce. Instead, I went south. I'm still not sure if it was a mistake. Really, I travelled south with the aim of Rome. I would find Caravaggio and beg to be his student, or at least find myself among his followers. He is where my problems started. Before I saw any of his work I had heard of the great Italian who created Reality. That his paintings ceased to be pictures or recreations, but so powerful they were real things themselves. When I saw one of these pieces at a lavish exhibition, for which I am indebted to my patron's sponsorship, I was struck. I spent months trying to recreate the style, and all of my production from this time was ridiculed and made tossed aside. But the need to follow this thread wouldn't leave. For a time, I hid my obsession and built my reputation, until last year, when I decided it was time to flee. But my journey didn't go as planned. I knew people's language would become insurmountably different as I went further south. I knew their dress and customs would change, too. But not this much. As I ventured further and further the changes became increasingly radical. Once I had crossed the mountains among a small band of mysterious men, I arrived in a penninsula undescribable. I remember everything being incredibly bright through the night, the constructs of Caravaggio showing themselves, and my travelling comrades bounding into a structure of armor which flew away. When I got to Rome it was unlike any city ever seen, heard, or dreamed. I don't bother telling anyone about this experience, for lack of my words, and lack of their ability to understand or believe me. It would be much more instrumental to present the manuscript of my friend David, whom I met there. He says he comes from the New World and is some sort of smith, a noble craft. Thank God for Guttenberg. The text is on my desk, right under the brush Margot just gave me. Though she wouldn't notice anything except a populated canvas.

## 2. A Collation of Entries Relating to Giasper's

### November 9, 2026

Hamilton, the reporter friend known through Monica, was telling us last night, at my third dinner party, about his time last year at the mysterious Giasper's. I'm lucky this intruiging anecdote of his stuck in my mind after it was plentily bathed in Syrah, and so today I write it at once:

It was winter of last year, the time when Giasper's is serving. Hamilton was creating a piece for the English Sensibilities section of the Neue Genfer Zeit. After mailing the required entry sum to Giasper's Treasury, an address only God knows how he obtained, he was taken from his hotel. It was about a week since his posting, a Friday, ripe for a feast. Men broke in, blinded his eyes and took him onto a bus full of others. He regained sight after being walked off the bus into what appeared to be a plane's jet bridge. He says that the ride to Giasper's is famously nauseating.

After the transportation, Hamilton's story gets blurry: in my mind, at least, and likely in the details he offered. But what I remember quite clearly that he said was that he thought the experience wasn't worth the rave praise it receives. He said he can eat plenty good seafood on the land, and didn't care for the ride down to the sea floor, nor the company of brahmins and their perverted actions at the dining tables.

This was the first I'd heard of Giasper's, though Monica acted as if it was old news. It wasn't news at all, though, since Giasper's Team caught wind of Hamilton's opinions, and paid him to be quiet.

Knowledge of this place seems limited to those who know things before the news, before the general public, before the word gets out: the reporters, the fashionable, and the wealthy. But now I know about it. Perhaps I'm now fashionable. And so I write this down. It's these kinds of things that make me await dinner party #4.

### November 17, 2026

Business is running at the bicycle foundry. Winter season. Giftcycle season. I hope these bikes spin longer than three months.

Sally came down to the brazing floor. She never interrupts my work. I knew she had a reason: "your mother has died."

Weird how I heard it, but yeah, that's the news.

Three notes Sally relayed, I assume from my uncle: (1) mom had money for me, (2) the bank is taking the money if they find out about it, (3) obviously, the bank is taking the house.

### November 19, 2026

I feel bad about not seeing her in a while. I would've, if I'd known about it. Everyone would've. She would've called me if she knew. A blind cancer like a coat of snow suffocates you while you can't even feel it. It's kind of like a therapy session that I write this. Shrink page, shrink pen, I'm shrinking into them, like somebody's hearing me, at least.

### November 30, 2026

Antonio, uncle, slips me an envelope at the collation. The collation at Rod's Deli, just into New Hampshire. It's a sleepy family: me, him, mom's friends from Montreal came down. Ilse se souviennent.

Lucky he kept in touch with her. Lucky he kept her amiable. I wish I did. I would've. Lived too far. And still, I would've, if anyone'd known.

I knew about her gambling problems, her mortgage problems, but I didn't know about ten thousand dollars. Lucky he did. No one else can.

At Rod's I took the stuffed squid. It got me thinking. A thinking food. Scissoring through rubber bulbs to where you think squid's brain would be. A real thinking food.

### December 4, 2026

Dinner party number four. Makes sense it's on the fourth. I made braised pork. Homophonous with much of my occupation. No one thought it was much funny. Well, they were mostly sad once I told them about my mother. I told Hamilton the best thing he could do was give me the address of Giasper's Treasury.
"Can you really afford that?"
"I'll see what I can do."
"Like I said, it's really not worth it, and of course, the address changes every season." But he couldn't offend the bereaved: "I'll see what I can do."

### December 13, 2026

Hamilton calls me as I'm on the brazing floor. "Of course, you'll have to mail from locally in Rome... The dinner costs $5,000." I thought about a fattened envelope at home and eased his concerns about my ability and readiness, and the worthiness of the endeavor.
"I need to do it. I just need a change of pace."
I asked Sally for my vacation time this holiday.

### December 30, 2026

My flight to Rome two days ago went smoothly, and yet I encountered turbulence upon landing. Besides giving a fateful parcel to the post, the past two days were filled with excessive sleep, unguided walks in an attempt to either curb or play out the overthinking of 'the right thing to be doing,' and after-dinner espressi that maybe either a wiser or more ignorant me would point to as the cause of it all. But I write on what I can sense in my soul to be the night before dining (for why else would tonight's events have happened), and all that shakiness has ceased. Not because of some property of the precipice I teeter on, but because of a man I met a few hours ago. I met him in the bar underneath my hotel. We bonded over the fact that we both couldn't speak Italian and could speak German. But language barriers weren't the primary cause of his isolation in the bar:

I didn't realize until we were well into conversation that he was some sort of bum. I hadn't noticed his tattered clothes, slight identifiable stench, or bag of bottles. But of course, we kept talking after I did. Some of what he said made little sense to me, and yet he had more humanity than most.
He said he can't work because they won't let him into the Galleria Borghese, where Caravaggio is, and that he can't return home because he'd have to tell his mother of his suffering. I gave him the best advice I could, and told him to tell his mother of his suffering, as I wish mine had to me.

This anecdote, of her cancer, somehow struck a chord with him. His tone changed. I really made him puzzled. I puzzled myself, too. What happened next is hard to rationalize in words, but it is what has now made me comfortable. I have become convinced our journeys are aligned, and I convinced him to join me to Giasper's tomorrow morning. He sleeps on the floor of my room as I write. When they come to fetch me, so long as there is the space, they surely wouldn't mind if comes along in exchange for my last $5,000. I hope, at least. Now I've bankrupted myself, but I've given everything more purpose.

### January 2, 2027

The object we seek, Thrill, has a certain way of making itself known in a way that is difficult to know: surprise, that is. They come in the night and rise us. I am stripped of belongings, made blind, and bound. This means, of course, I could not bring a pen, a notebook: I write this in the afterglow. Thankfully, my friend is included in the constrainment after not words but gesticulation among us and monetary capitulation. They guide us down the stairs, out the door, and onto what feels like bus full of others. No one speaks but I can sense a sea of anxiety.

The binds and the blinding get removed, after that initial transportation, on something like a jet bridge. The cohort is finalized and freed, relatively, on the bridge, and we walk on to the vehicle decidely without any glimpse outside.
"Welcome to the plunger. Please take your seats and buckle as we will descend shortly," the loudspeaker repeats in Italian and English.

I am finally able to get a look at my company for today's feast. As we sit down I peer around. Many are well-kept, wealthy internationals. Some, obvious Romans. Some are more bitten-into people, looking like they reside on the edge of things. The staff, directing the flow of folks down the central aisle are neatly sartorial-clad: dark woolen material with red and gold stripes forms their perfect silhouettes. They are men and women with neatly-done, coarse dark hair and olive skin. The seats are similar to their dress, red with purple stripes. I am next to the wall with a covered inlet of a window, my friend to my right. The man next to him, in liederhosen, offers: "this can't possibly hold anything to the food in Tirol."
The person next to that man, next to the aisle, wearing something with many buttons: "No, no, it will dispel any earthly attachments to foods, truly ruining," implying she regularly makes the plunge.

After a period of chatter, building a suspenseful attitude among us all, the staff gives a loud, verbal "hush!" Everyone quiets and there is a lurch forward with which we start moving and the cabin lighting becomes dim. After a few minutes the window next to me as well as all of those along the side of the vessel open in a synchronized clatter. A wave of "ahh"s come out through the cabin followed by another "hush," and "please respect the solace sought in this moment by all of our comradery." While there had been a rolling sensation of the vehicle along the ground, we soon experience a slight lift, after which spotlights start to shine downward from above each seating position. They indicate our attention to the floor, which then exposes a glass window under which the opaque chasis making up the bottom of the vessel recedes to the sides with a final clatter. We are thereby exposed to the sights of the Sea.

I tell Mark that this is cancer: humans that will only eat the grossly extravagant, do anything for it, and travel to the bottom of the Sea for the same sustenance that the simplest of folks fulfill via barley and farro. Misery led to Purposelessness and so cancer, the Crab, is what we eat. I had to eat cancer to know cancer. I knew what was in store, planned for it to be, and yet the nature of Thrill is that you cannot brace for the traumament. The reason I did it: it brought me closer to my mother.

The reason Shelby did it is because she doesn't eat anything but Giasper's, once a month.
The reason Giusseppe did it is because he thinks it's more ethical than trawl-caught fare.
The reason Gunther von Konstanz did it is because a woman he fancies, Esmerelda, does it every now and then.
The reason Bishop Garibaldi did it is because he feels the Church is under too much scrutiny nowadays.
The reason Esmerelda did it is for the taste and the texture.
The reason Hamilton did it is to write a story and get paid.
The reason Markus von Hinterland did it is to experience Reality.

## 3. The Return

This is my final walk outside the shack. I walk by brownish tall grasses, through fog, and down the hill, in an act of deserting: deserting the paintbrush in the shack, deserting that old home itself. I spent so much time reading David's manuscript. I read it almost every day. I finally act on what I knew for a long time: I need to talk to my mother, show her my clear skin and the lack of hoarseness in my voice, explain where I was and who I am. I must tell her the truth, that I have not consumption but cancer, the problem of the Crab.

I ruminate as I uncover, step by step:
In the past month I've made a few charcoal sketches of the dome and the plunger, but they don't feel like art. Evidently, neither do they to Margot, who wouldn't believe I'm recovered until I produce a true painting. The sketches aren't artistic to me, but more like a map, a practical creation as an attempt to physically jog my memory of those times. Jog it to what end I do not exactly know, except that I feel the only thing that seems to hold real value lies in my mind somewhere near there. Upon this consideration of my sketching it occurs to me that this seems like a description of art. But no, the sketches do not feel like art. I feel like an artist but the sketches do not feel like art. The sketches are hollow and slightly disgusting, but their creation seemed necessary in order to get at something possibly like art. But after their produciton I see clearly that no, they are not art. I see now why all of my elders wanted me not to pursue Caravaggio. Now I have thrown into question all of what is real and true. But I'm still not sure if I'm any worse off.

And now, for you the reader, I have one last confession: I never was a painter, and a painter never was me. Nor do I live in the 17th Century. And yet everything I have said is true and decent. I hope you can believe me.
